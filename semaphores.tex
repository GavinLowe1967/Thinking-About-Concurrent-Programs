\section{Summary}

In this chapter we have studied semaphores.  A binary semaphore has two
states, \emph{up} and \emph{down}.  The |up| operation requires the semaphore
to be in the \emph{down} state, and puts it into the \emph{up} state.  The
|down| operation waits until the semaphore is in the \emph{up} state, and puts
it into the \emph{down} state. 

A semaphores can be used for mutual exclusion.  Such a semaphore is initially
in the \emph{up} state.  A thread performs |down| before entering the critical
region, and performs |up| when leaving the critical region.

A semaphore can also be used for signalling.  Such a semaphore is initially in
the \emph{up} state.  A thread performs |up| to send a signal.  A thread
performs |down| to wait to receive a signal.  The semaphore must be in the
|down| state before the |up| is performed: otherwise, our implementation would
throw an exception; with other implementations, the signal would be lost.

Signalling semaphores are often used in combination with a mutex semaphore
that protects shared variables.  Or sometimes, the combination of several
semaphores provides mutual exclusion: it is worth being clear about how mutual
exclusion is achieved in such cases.

Semaphores have some similarities to |Condition|s, in that both can be used
for waiting and signalling.  The main difference is that a semaphore has a
state; this means that if a thread performs a signal (|up|) before a thread is
ready to receive it (via a |down|), the signal is not lost (assuming the
semaphore is in the \emph{down} state).

An important technique is that of \emph{passing the baton}.  The current
thread is often in the best position to decide which thread (or maybe one of a
group of threads) should run next.  A thread that waits to receive the baton
should release the mutex: otherwise it will deadlock the system.  By contrast,
a thread that passes the baton should leave the mutex down; the thread that
receives the signal takes over ownership of the mutex.  

We have concentrated on binary semaphores, which have just two states.  By
contrast, a counting semaphore has a non-negative integer as its state.  The
state of the semaphore can be thought of as the number of permits to perform
some operation.  An |up| operation provides another permit, and a |down|
operation consumes a permit.
